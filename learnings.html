How Peek works

Four agents, each with one job, passing work down a chain. The Scout's sources travel with the draft to every downstream agent — so faithfulness gets re-checked at every step, not just at the end.

The Scout's research is the single source of truth. Anything a later agent can't tie back to it doesn't make the final page — that constraint, enforced at every step, is the whole design.

Measurement

The tool's whole promise is "faithful, cited, no hallucination" — so the eval grades exactly that. A separate model scores every brief 1–5, seeing only the brief and the Scout's sources (the same fact base the pipeline had). I ran ~10 companies through it, including deliberately thin ones and one that doesn't exist.

Faithfulness
Does every claim trace to a cited source? No unsupported inference, no "citation laundering" (citing a source but saying more than it does).
Citation accuracy
Does each [N] point to a source that genuinely supports the exact claim?
Specificity
Concrete numbers and dates where the sources have them — not "many" or "popular."
Recommendation
A usable strategic read for a market entrant — threat, opening, move, watch-trigger — grounded in cited facts.
Guardrail
On thin or fake companies, does it refuse and flag rather than invent? The sharpest anti-hallucination test.

Scores are a strong signal, not gospel — so I validated the judge by hand-checking its findings against the sources before trusting it at scale.

Learnings

Four things that stuck with me.

01

Hallucination is real — you reduce it, you don't eliminate it

I built the pipeline specifically to stop the model from making things up: the Writer is told to use only what the Scout actually found, and the Critic's whole job is to catch anything the sources don't support. It still hallucinated sometimes. That was the humbling part — even with layers of guardrails, you can't fully remove it. What you can do is drive the odds down, and make sure that when something does slip through, it's visible rather than buried. Every rule, review step, and eval made it rarer; none of them made it impossible. I stopped designing for "impossible" and started designing for "rare, and caught."

02

A PRD matters more when you build with AI, not less

AI moves fast, so it's tempting to skip the planning and just start generating. I found the opposite. Writing a clear PRD — and going back and forth with the AI until we'd agreed on every detail — was what held the project together from end to end. And honestly: I didn't fully understand every single thing the AI proposed along the way. Nobody does. The PRD was the shared source of truth that kept us aimed at the same goal — even for the parts that went deeper than my own expertise. It's the difference between directing the work and just watching it happen.

03

Send your strongest model where judgment lives

Not every step needs your most capable model. But the parts that call for real reasoning and judgment — deciding whether a claim is genuinely supported, critiquing a draft, making a strategic call — are where a more robust model earns its cost. My A/B test made it concrete: the two models tied on the mechanical scores, and the gap opened up exactly on the faithfulness and citation calls, the judgment-heavy ones. Spend model capability where judgment lives, not uniformly.

04

Better to leave it blank than to sound good and be wrong

My instinct was that a complete brief is a better brief. It isn't. An output that says "this needs more investigation" is more useful than one that fills the gap with something that sounds right but isn't grounded — or a recommendation that reads well but no one can actually act on. A confident answer that's wrong, or polish dressed up as insight, quietly erodes trust in everything next to it. A flagged blank beats a nice-sounding guess — so Peek is built to leave the gap and say so.
